Background
Every tele-call and field visit assumes that the phone number or
address on file still reaches the borrower. In practice, a large
share of dial attempts and visits go to contact points that are
dead, recycled to a new subscriber, belong to a relative or former
employer, or were wrong from the start. This is usually
discovered slowly: a number rings out for weeks before anyone
acts, or an address is visited three times before it is marked
untraceable.
 
Skip-tracing is expensive and is normally triggered by a fixed
attempt count rather than by evidence. As a result, it comes too
late for the accounts that needed it and is wasted on accounts
where the borrower was simply not answering.
 
The question that matters, whether a specific contact point still
reaches a specific borrower, is rarely modelled on its own. It is
usually folded into an account-level "contactability" score,
which hides the actual decision: which number to dial, which
address to visit, and when to stop and trace.

Objective
A model that predicts, for every phone number and address on
file, how likely it is to reach the borrower, that is, to produce a
right-party contact (RPC) in tele or field. A prioritisation layer
then decides, for each account, whether to keep trying, switch to
another contact point or channel, or trigger a skip-trace, with
skip-traces ranked by the expected recovery value of finding the
borrower.
 
This is a prediction made before the call or visit. Checking
during a call whether the person who answered is actually the
borrower is a separate identity-verification problem and is out of
scope.
 
Key things to solve:
 
- Building labels. True health is never observed directly. A
right-party contact is only recorded when someone dialled,
the borrower answered and the agent logged it correctly,
and many contact points have never been tested at all.
- Avoiding versus invalid. A borrower who won't answer and
a dead number look similar on the surface but need
opposite actions: switch channel for the first, trace for the
second. Separating them is the central modelling problem.
- Recycled numbers. Telecom operators reassign inactive
numbers to new subscribers without any explicit signal.
Calling a recycled number wastes effort and risks
disclosing the debt to a stranger, which breaches the Fair
Practices Code.
- Health changes over time. The model needs to estimate how
long a contact point is likely to stay valid, so that
confidence falls as evidence ages. A survival or hidden-
state model fits this better than a one-off classifier.
- When to trace. A skip-trace is worth it when the expected
recovery from finding a valid contact point, given the
amount outstanding and the chance of recovery once
reached, is higher than the trace cost. This is a value-of-
information decision, not a fixed attempt count.
- Feedback loops. Once the score decides which numbers get
dialled, it also shapes the data it learns from. A small,
explicit exploration budget keeps low-scored contact points
from being starved of the evidence that would correct them.
Key signals
- Telephony: attempt history by time slot, ring duration,
answer and immediate-hangup patterns, network responses
("switched off", "not reachable", "does not exist"), and time
since the last right-party contact on the number.
- Dispositions and remarks: wrong-number and third-party
dispositions, agent remarks ("phone uthaya bhai ne",
"number band hai 2 mahine se") and voice-bot transcripts
showing who answered.
- Shared contact points: the same number or address
appearing across several borrowers, lenders or co-borrower
records, which often points to a reference, shared or
recycled contact.
- Field visits: outcomes at the address, GPS dwell at check-in,
and the difference between "locked premises" and "nobody
of that name here".
- Record history: where the contact point came from (KYC at
origination, a later update, the bureau, the borrower during
a call), how old it is, and whether it was ever confirmed by
a contact that led to a payment.
Suggested phasing
The core question and the final decision are the same for both
channels, so this stays one problem. The data is not: phone data
is dense, with many attempts a day, while field data is sparse and
costly, with a few visits a month. The work is therefore phased:
 
- Weeks 1 to 6: phone numbers. Build health scoring for
phone contact points, the avoiding-versus-invalid split and
the recycled-number check, and integrate with the CN
dialer and the compliance suppression list.
- Weeks 7 to 12: addresses and field evidence. Add address
health, bring field visit outcomes into the same model, use
location confidence from Problem Statement 3, and switch
on the account-level skip-trace decision across all contact
points.
Expected outcome
A right-party contact prediction service running inside the CN
platform, integrated as follows:
 
- Input from CN: dial attempt logs and network responses
from the CN dialer, dispositions and remarks, voice-bot
transcripts, field visit outcomes from the CN field app, and
contact-point source and history from account records.
- CN dialer: dial lists ordered by contact-point health, so the
best number for each account is tried first and dead
numbers drop out.
- CN campaign engine: the recommended action for each
account (continue, switch contact point, switch channel,
trace), with a reason code, applied as campaign rules.
- CN skip-trace queue: accounts ranked by the expected value
of tracing, replacing the fixed attempt-count trigger.
- CN compliance controls: a suppression list for recycled and
third-party numbers, so the debt is never discussed with the
wrong person.
- CN visit planner: address health used in visit selection,
alongside location confidence from Problem Statement 3.
Key metrics
- Right-party contact rate per thousand dial attempts and per
hundred field visits, compared with current rules
- AUC and calibration of the right-party contact prediction for
each contact point
- Attempts and visits spent on invalid contact points before
action is taken
- Skip-trace hit rate and recovery from traced accounts,
compared with the fixed attempt-count rule
- Third-party disclosure incidents (target: zero)
Constraints
The debt is never disclosed to a third party, as required by the
Fair Practices Code. Contact data from bureaus and references is
used only for the purpose it was collected for, under DPDP.
Skip-trace sources must be lawful and auditable. Scores are
refreshed daily so that they are ready for outbound planning.
 
 
Problem Statement 3: Address Geocoder That Learns from Field Visits
Background
Field collections depends on finding the borrower, and that
depends on an address that is often a description rather than a
location: "near Hanuman temple, behind ration shop, 2nd cross,
Kamakshipalya". Commercial geocoders place addresses like
this at the pincode or locality centre, which in tier-2, tier-3 and
rural areas can be hundreds of metres or even several kilometres
from the actual house. Agents spend much of their day searching
and asking neighbours. Eventually they mark accounts "address
not traceable", including some where the borrower lives exactly
where the address says.
 
Meanwhile, the answer already exists for a large share of
accounts: the GPS location where an agent actually met the
borrower on an earlier visit. Today that sits in visit logs and
never flows back into the address record.
